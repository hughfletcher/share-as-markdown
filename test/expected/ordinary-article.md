_Volunteers plant saplings on a former parking lot in Leeds._

On a former parking lot squeezed between a bus depot and a row of terraced houses, more than six hundred saplings are growing in a space no larger than a tennis court. Two years ago the ground was asphalt. Today it is loud with birds.

The patch is one of hundreds of so-called _tiny forests_ now appearing in cities across Europe and Asia, following a method developed by the Japanese botanist [Akira Miyawaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Miyawaki) in the 1970s.

## How the method works

Miyawaki forests are planted densely, with three to five native saplings per square metre. Organisers typically follow the same basic steps:

1.  Survey the remnants of natural vegetation nearby to choose native species.
2.  Improve the soil with locally sourced organic matter.
3.  Plant a mix of canopy, sub-tree, shrub and ground-cover species close together.
4.  Water and weed for the first two or three years, then leave the forest alone.

Because the young trees compete for light, they grow quickly upward. Proponents say the forests become self-sustaining within three years.

> “People assume you need acres. You need about the area of six parking spaces and a lot of enthusiastic neighbours.”

## What the evidence says

Researchers are more cautious. A 2023 review found that growth claims are often based on small samples, though the forests do measurably increase insect diversity compared with mown grass.

| Measure | Mown grass | Tiny forest (3 years) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insect species recorded | 14 | 41 |
| Surface temperature, July midday | 38 °C | 29 °C |
| Maintenance visits per year | 26 | 4 |

### Unanswered questions

-   How well do the forests survive long droughts?
-   Do they store meaningful amounts of carbon at this scale?
-   Who maintains them once the founding volunteers move on?

For now, the people of the old parking lot are not waiting for answers. “It’s ours,” said one resident. “That’s the point.”
